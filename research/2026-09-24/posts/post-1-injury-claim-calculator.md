# Injury Claim Calculator: How Insurers Value Your Claim

*By Settlebrook Editorial*

When an insurance adjuster looks at your personal injury claim, they're weighing your bills, your wage loss, your medical records, and your share of fault to land on a number. You can't see that process from the outside, which is why the offer that lands in your inbox can feel like it was pulled out of thin air.

You can build your own estimate. Our free [injury claim calculator](https://www.settlebrook.com/pain-and-suffering-calculator/) uses the two most widely used ways of putting a number on pain and suffering, the multiplier method and the per diem method, so you can see roughly where your claim might land before you accept anything.

This guide walks through what actually goes into that number: how economic and non-economic damages are added up, how your own share of fault can shrink the total, and what adjusters are really looking at when they read your file. We'll also work through one full example with real math, so you can see exactly how the pieces fit together.

## What an Injury Claim Calculator Actually Does

A personal injury claim is made up of two very different kinds of losses. The first kind is economic — your medical bills, your lost paychecks, the money that actually left your pocket or your future paycheck. The second kind is non-economic — pain, physical limitation, disruption to your life — and it doesn't come with a receipt.

An injury claim calculator estimates that second, harder-to-price category using one of two standard methods:

- **The multiplier method** takes your economic damages and multiplies them by a factor tied to how serious the injury is.
- **The per diem method** assigns a dollar value to each day you were affected by the injury and multiplies it by the number of recovery days.

Neither method is a legal guarantee of what you'll receive. Both are estimating tools that give you a reasoned starting point before negotiation begins. For the full breakdown of how we built ours, see our [methodology page](https://www.settlebrook.com/methodology/).

## The Building Blocks of a Claim Value

### Special Damages (the economic side)

Special damages are the losses you can document with paperwork:

- Medical bills already incurred
- Future medical care, when a doctor has projected it
- Lost wages from time already missed
- Future lost wages or reduced earning capacity, when supported by medical or vocational evidence
- Property damage, most often vehicle repair or replacement costs

In Settlebrook's calculator, we add the first four of these together into what we call the **multiplier base** (medical bills + future medical + lost wages + future lost wages), and we add property damage on top of that separately to get **special damages**.

### General Damages (pain and suffering)

General damages cover pain, suffering, emotional distress, and loss of enjoyment of life. There's no invoice for this, so the multiplier method converts your multiplier base into a dollar figure using a factor tied to injury severity. Our calculator uses:

| Severity | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Minor | 1.5 |
| Moderate | 2.5 |
| Serious | 3.5 |
| Severe | 4.5 |
| Catastrophic | 5.0 |

A sprained wrist that heals in six weeks sits at the low end. A spinal injury with permanent restrictions sits at the high end. Everything else falls somewhere in between, and reasonable people — including adjusters and juries — can disagree on exactly where.

### The Per Diem Alternative

Instead of a multiplier, the per diem method picks a daily rate (often anchored to your own daily earnings) and multiplies it by the number of days you were in active recovery:

**Per diem pain and suffering = daily rate × recovery days**

This method is easiest to apply to injuries with a clear, bounded recovery period — a broken bone that heals on a set timeline, for example — and less well for injuries with permanent or uncertain effects, where a multiplier is more common.

## Worked Example: How the Math Comes Together

Here's a complete, fully hypothetical example using Settlebrook's exact formula, step by step. None of these figures represent an actual case or a typical outcome — they're just numbers chosen to show the math clearly.

**The facts (all hypothetical):**

- Medical bills to date: $18,000
- Future medical care: $7,000
- Lost wages to date: $6,000
- Future lost wages: $4,000
- Property damage: $5,000
- Injury severity: Moderate (multiplier 2.5)
- Plaintiff fault: 20%

**Step 1 — Multiplier base**
$18,000 + $7,000 + $6,000 + $4,000 = **$35,000**

**Step 2 — Special damages**
$35,000 (multiplier base) + $5,000 (property damage) = **$40,000**

**Step 3 — Pain and suffering**
$35,000 × 2.5 = **$87,500**

**Step 4 — Total (pre-fault-adjustment)**
$40,000 + $87,500 = **$127,500**

**Step 5 — Fault-adjusted total**
$127,500 × (100 − 20) / 100 = $127,500 × 0.80 = **$102,000**

So in this hypothetical, a claim worth $127,500 before any fault dispute lands at **$102,000** once a 20% comparative-fault finding is applied.

**For comparison, the per diem alternative:**
If we instead valued pain and suffering at a daily rate of $150 over 240 recovery days:
$150 × 240 = **$36,000** (versus $87,500 under the multiplier method)

That gap is the whole reason it's worth running both methods. The multiplier and per diem approaches can produce very different numbers for the same injury, and knowing both gives you a realistic range instead of a single figure.

## What an Insurance Adjuster Actually Weighs

The math above is only the starting point. Before an adjuster ever applies a multiplier, they're evaluating your file against a handful of practical factors that push the number up or down:

- **Documentation quality.** Consistent, itemized medical records and bills that clearly tie treatment to the incident carry more weight than gaps or vague notes.
- **Treatment gaps.** A large gap between the incident and your first medical visit, or long pauses in treatment, gives an adjuster a reason to argue the injury wasn't serious or wasn't caused by the incident.
- **Policy limits.** An insurer will not pay more than the at-fault party's available coverage, regardless of how strong the claim is, which is why policy limits are one of the first things an adjuster checks.
- **Liability disputes.** If fault is contested, the adjuster is pricing in the cost and risk of litigation, not just the injury itself.
- **Venue.** The jurisdiction where a case would be tried affects an adjuster's internal valuation, since local jury tendencies and court procedures vary.

None of this is arbitrary once you know it's there — it's a checklist, and it's worth building your file with that checklist in mind.

## How Comparative Fault Changes the Number

Every state applies some version of a fault-sharing rule, and the rule your state uses can swing your final number as much as the injury itself does. There are four main categories.

### Pure Comparative Fault — New York

Under a pure comparative fault rule, you can recover damages even if you were mostly at fault — your recovery is just reduced by your percentage of fault, with no cutoff. New York applies this rule under [N.Y. CPLR § 1411](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CVP/1411), which states that a claimant's culpable conduct "shall not bar recovery," and instead the damages otherwise recoverable "shall be diminished in the proportion" that the claimant's fault bears to the total fault that caused the injury. In theory, a claimant who is 90% at fault can still recover the remaining 10% of their damages.

### Modified Comparative Fault, 50% Bar — Georgia

Some states cut off recovery once the plaintiff's fault reaches a certain share. Georgia bars recovery once the plaintiff is 50% or more responsible. Under [O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33](https://law.justia.com/codes/georgia/title-51/chapter-12/article-2/section-51-12-33/), a plaintiff who is found 50 percent or more responsible for their own injury is not entitled to recover any damages at all.

### Modified Comparative Fault, 51% Bar — Florida

Other states set the cutoff one point higher, so a plaintiff can be exactly 50% at fault and still recover. Florida moved to this model in 2023. Under [Fla. Stat. § 768.81(6)](https://www.flsenate.gov/laws/statutes/2023/768.81), "any party found to be greater than 50 percent at fault for his or her own harm may not recover any damages," which means a plaintiff at 50% fault or less can still recover a reduced award, while a plaintiff at 51% or more recovers nothing.

### Contributory Negligence — Maryland

A small number of jurisdictions still use the strictest rule of all: if you bear any fault, even a small amount, you recover nothing. Maryland is one of them. In *Coleman v. Soccer Association of Columbia* (Md. July 9, 2013), the Maryland high court declined to replace this rule with comparative fault, writing that it would "decline to abrogate Maryland's long-established common law principle of contributory negligence," and left any change in that doctrine to the state legislature. You can read the full opinion on the [Maryland Judiciary's website](https://www.mdcourts.gov/opinions/coa/2013/9a12.pdf).

Because this single rule can turn a six-figure claim into zero, it's worth knowing which category your state falls into before you accept — or dismiss — any settlement offer.

## Using the Calculator the Right Way

Run the numbers before you talk to an adjuster, not after you've already gotten an offer. Once you have a range, that range becomes your anchor in negotiation — it's a lot easier to push back on a lowball offer when you know roughly where the math should land and why.

It also helps to run more than one scenario. Try your case at a lower severity rating and a higher one, and try it with a couple of different fault percentages, so you understand the full range of reasonable outcomes rather than a single number.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is an injury claim calculator the same as what an insurance company uses?**
It uses the two most common estimating methods, the multiplier method and the per diem method, but a calculator can't account for every fact-specific detail an adjuster or attorney will weigh, like documentation quality or a disputed liability picture. Treat the output as a starting estimate, not a guaranteed number.

**What multiplier should I use for my injury?**
Multipliers scale with severity and permanence: minor, short-term injuries sit around 1.5, while catastrophic, permanent injuries can reach 5.0. If you're unsure where your injury falls, it's reasonable to run the calculator at two or three different severity levels to see the full range.

**Does comparative fault apply even if the other driver was mostly at fault?**
Yes. In comparative fault states, any percentage of fault assigned to you reduces your recovery by that percentage, even if it's small. In modified comparative fault states, reaching the state's bar (50% in Georgia, more than 50% in Florida) eliminates recovery entirely. In contributory negligence jurisdictions like Maryland, any fault at all can bar recovery.

**Why did the insurance company's number come out lower than my calculator estimate?**
The most common reasons are a disputed severity rating, treatment gaps the adjuster is using to discount causation, a policy limits ceiling that caps what they can offer regardless of the claim's value, or a fault percentage they're assigning to you that you don't agree with. Ask the adjuster directly which of these is driving their number.

**Should I accept the insurance company's first offer?**
A first offer is often an opening position rather than a final number. Running your own estimate first — and understanding your state's comparative fault rule — gives you a factual basis to negotiate rather than accepting or rejecting an offer based on instinct alone.
